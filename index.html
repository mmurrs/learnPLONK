Module 0

The Big Picture

Before diving into PLONK, let's understand why it exists and where it fits.

What Are Zero-Knowledge Proofs?

A zero-knowledge proof (ZKP) lets one party (the prover) convince another party (the verifier) that a statement is true — without revealing any information beyond the truth of the statement itself.

The Classic Analogy: Ali Baba's Cave

Imagine a cave with a circular path and a locked door in the middle. You want to prove you know the secret word that opens the door, but you don't want to reveal the word.

  1. You enter the cave and take a random path (left or right).
  2. The verifier, waiting outside, shouts which side they want you to come out from.
  3. If you know the secret word, you can always come out the correct side (opening the door if needed).
  4. After many rounds, the verifier is convinced you know the word — but never learns the word itself.

ZKPs have three essential properties:

Completeness

If the statement is true and both parties follow the protocol, the verifier will be convinced.

Soundness

If the statement is false, no cheating prover can convince the verifier (except with negligible probability).

Zero-Knowledge

The verifier learns nothing beyond the fact that the statement is true.

The ZK Proof Landscape

The zero-knowledge proof ecosystem has evolved into several major proof system families:

SNARKs

Succinct Non-interactive ARguments of Knowledge

  • Small proof size (constant or logarithmic)
  • Fast verification
  • Typically requires a trusted setup
  • Examples: Groth16, PLONK, Marlin

STARKs

Scalable Transparent ARguments of Knowledge

  • No trusted setup (transparent)
  • Larger proof sizes
  • Post-quantum secure
  • Examples: StarkWare's systems

PLONK

Permutations over Lagrange-bases for Oecumenical Noninteractive arguments of Knowledge

  • Universal trusted setup (one ceremony works for all circuits)
  • Flexible gate design
  • Foundation for many modern ZK systems
  • Used by: zkSync, Aztec, Polygon zkEVM, Scroll

Why Does This Matter?

Zero-knowledge proofs are transforming multiple domains:

Blockchain Scaling

ZK-rollups batch thousands of transactions and prove their validity with a single proof. This is how systems like zkSync and Polygon zkEVM achieve massive throughput increases.

Privacy

Prove you meet certain criteria (e.g., "I'm over 18", "I have sufficient funds") without revealing the underlying data. Used in protocols like Zcash and Aztec.

Verifiable Computation

Offload computation to an untrusted party and verify the result cheaply. The verifier does far less work than re-executing the computation.

Identity & Credentials

Prove properties about your identity (citizenship, qualifications) without exposing personal information. A building block for self-sovereign identity.

What is PLONK?

Understanding the core ideas behind PLONK and how it represents computation.

PLONK at a Glance

PLONK was introduced in 2019 by Ariel Gabizon, Zachary J. Williamson, and Oana Ciobotaru. It's a universal zk-SNARK that has become one of the most widely adopted proving systems in the blockchain ecosystem.

The name is a (somewhat forced) acronym:

Permutations
Lagrange-bases
Oecumenical
Noninteractive
Knowledge

"Oecumenical" here means universal — the same setup can be used for different circuits. This was a major improvement over earlier systems where each circuit needed its own trusted setup ceremony.

How PLONK Represents Computation

PLONK turns any computation into something that can be proven in zero-knowledge through a process called arithmetization. Here's the pipeline:

1

Computation

Start with a program or statement you want to prove (e.g., "I know x such that x³ + x + 5 = 35")

↓
2

Arithmetic Circuit

Express the computation as a circuit of addition and multiplication gates, connected by wires

↓
3

Constraint System

Convert the circuit into polynomial equations. Each gate becomes a constraint, and wire connections become copy constraints

↓
4

Polynomial IOP

The prover commits to polynomials that encode the witness (the secret inputs) and proves they satisfy all constraints

↓
5

ZK Proof

Using a polynomial commitment scheme (KZG), produce a compact proof that the verifier can check quickly

Arithmetic Circuits & Gates

In PLONK, computation is broken down into gates. Each gate takes inputs from the left (a) and right (b) wires and produces an output (c) wire.

A general PLONK gate enforces this constraint:

qL·a + qR·b + qO·c + qM·(a·b) + qC = 0

The q values are selector polynomials chosen by the circuit designer. Different selectors create different gate types:

Gate TypeqLqRqOqMqCConstraint
Addition11-100a + b - c = 0
Multiplication00-110a·b - c = 0
Constant1000-ka = k
Boolean-10010a·a - a = 0 (a is 0 or 1)

Copy Constraints (The "P" in PLONK)

When wires from one gate feed into another, we need copy constraints to ensure the same value appears in both places. This is where permutation arguments come in.

PLONK uses a clever permutation argument to check that wire values are consistent across gates. Instead of checking each connection individually, it encodes all wire connections as a single permutation and verifies it using a grand product argument.

Key Insight: The permutation argument is what makes PLONK "PLONK" — it's an elegant way to enforce that values are correctly wired between gates, and it works for any circuit topology.

Module 2

Math Foundations

The mathematical building blocks that make PLONK possible.

Finite Fields

All arithmetic in PLONK happens over a finite field — a set of numbers where addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division all wrap around a prime number p.

Think of it like clock arithmetic

In a field with p = 7: 5 + 4 = 9 mod 7 = 2. Every operation stays within {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. This bounded arithmetic is essential for cryptographic security — there's no "overflow" leaking information.

Why finite fields? They give us exact arithmetic (no floating-point errors), efficient computation, and the algebraic structure needed for polynomial commitments and pairings.

Polynomials: The Language of PLONK

Polynomials are the core data structure of PLONK. Everything — the circuit, the witness, the constraints — gets encoded as polynomials.

The Schwartz-Zippel Lemma: If two different polynomials of degree at most d are evaluated at a random point, they agree with probability at most d/|F|. Since our field is huge, this is negligibly small. This means: checking polynomial equality at a random point is almost as good as checking it everywhere.

This is the fundamental insight that makes SNARKs succinct — instead of checking constraints for every gate, we check polynomial identities at random points.

Lagrange Interpolation (The "L" in PLONK)

Given a set of points, Lagrange interpolation finds the unique polynomial that passes through all of them.

PLONK uses a special set of evaluation points called roots of unity — the n-th roots of 1 in the finite field. These form a multiplicative subgroup with nice algebraic properties that make the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) efficient.

Roots of unity ω satisfy ωn = 1. The set {1, ω, ω², ..., ωn-1} has a cyclic structure that enables:

  • Efficient FFT: Convert between coefficient and evaluation form in O(n log n)
  • Simple vanishing polynomial: ZH(x) = xn - 1 vanishes on all roots of unity
  • Clean Lagrange bases: The Lagrange basis polynomials have simple closed forms

This structure is critical for PLONK's efficiency — without it, polynomial operations would be prohibitively expensive.

Polynomial Commitment Schemes (KZG)

A polynomial commitment scheme lets you commit to a polynomial and later prove evaluations of it without revealing the entire polynomial. PLONK uses the Kate-Zaverucha-Goldberg (KZG) scheme.

1

Commit

The prover computes a commitment C = [f(s)]1 using the structured reference string (SRS) from the trusted setup

↓
2

Open

To prove f(z) = y, the prover computes a quotient polynomial q(x) = (f(x) - y) / (x - z) and commits to it

↓
3

Verify

The verifier checks the opening using an elliptic curve pairing: e(C - [y]1, [1]2) = e([q]1, [s - z]2)

Why KZG is powerful: Commitments are a single elliptic curve point (48 bytes). Opening proofs are also a single point. Verification requires just 2 pairing operations regardless of polynomial degree. This is what makes PLONK proofs succinct.

Elliptic Curve Pairings

Pairings are a special mathematical operation on elliptic curves that PLONK uses for verification. A pairing e maps two curve points to a target group element with a key property:

e([a]1, [b]2) = e([1]1, [1]2)ab

This bilinearity lets the verifier check polynomial relationships without knowing the secret evaluation point s from the trusted setup. PLONK typically uses the BN254 or BLS12-381 curves.

Module 3

The PLONK Protocol

The three phases of PLONK: Setup, Prove, and Verify.

Phase 1: Universal Setup

The setup generates a Structured Reference String (SRS) from a secret value s (the "toxic waste" that must be destroyed).

The SRS contains powers of s encoded on the elliptic curve:

SRS = { [1]1, [s]1, [s²]1, ..., [sn]1, [1]2, [s]2 }

The key innovation: this SRS is universal. Unlike Groth16 where you need a new setup for each circuit, PLONK's SRS works for any circuit up to size n. This is done through a powers-of-tau ceremony where many participants contribute randomness — the setup is secure as long as at least one participant is honest.

Phase 2: Proving

The prover goes through several rounds to generate the proof, using the Fiat-Shamir heuristic to make the protocol non-interactive (challenges come from hashing transcripts instead of an interactive verifier).

Round 1: Wire Polynomials

The prover constructs polynomials a(x), b(x), c(x) encoding the wire values (the witness) using Lagrange interpolation over the roots of unity. Blinding factors are added for zero-knowledge. The prover commits to these polynomials.

Round 2: Permutation Polynomial

Using challenges β and γ from the Fiat-Shamir transcript, the prover constructs the permutation accumulator polynomial z(x) that encodes the grand product argument for copy constraints. The prover commits to z(x).

Round 3: Quotient Polynomial

Using challenge α, the prover combines all constraints (gate constraints + permutation constraints) into one big polynomial, divides by the vanishing polynomial ZH(x) = xn - 1, and splits the quotient into parts. The prover commits to the quotient parts.

Round 4: Evaluations

Using evaluation challenge ζ, the prover evaluates all relevant polynomials at ζ (and z(x) at ζω) and provides these as field elements.

Round 5: Opening Proofs

Using challenge ν, the prover constructs linearized opening proofs (KZG) that let the verifier check all evaluations in a batch.

Phase 3: Verification

The verifier reconstructs the challenges from the proof transcript (Fiat-Shamir), then checks:

  1. Gate constraint: The committed polynomials satisfy the arithmetic gate equation at the evaluation point
  2. Permutation constraint: The permutation accumulator starts at 1 and the grand product relation holds
  3. Polynomial commitments: The evaluations are consistent with the commitments (via KZG pairing checks)
Verification is fast: The verifier only needs to perform a constant number of elliptic curve operations and pairings — independent of the circuit size. This is what makes PLONK (and SNARKs in general) powerful for on-chain verification.

Self-Check: Did You Absorb It?

After working through all three parts, you should be able to answer these questions. Click each to reveal a brief answer.

ZH(x) = xn - 1 is zero on all roots of unity. If a constraint polynomial is divisible by ZH, it means the constraints are satisfied at every gate position. The prover demonstrates this by providing the quotient t(x).

The permutation accumulator z(x) starts at 1 and accumulates a product of ratios. If the wire values respect the permutation (i.e., connected wires have the same value), the product telescopes and z ends at 1 again. Any mismatch causes z to diverge.

Without blinding, the committed polynomials would be fully determined by the witness, and a malicious verifier could potentially extract the secret values. Blinding factors add random terms that don't affect the constraint checks (they vanish on the evaluation domain) but make the polynomials information-theoretically hiding.

Linearization is a technique to reduce the verifier's work. Instead of the verifier needing to compute products of committed polynomials (which would require expensive operations), the prover provides evaluations that let the verifier reduce everything to checking a single polynomial commitment opening. It turns a non-linear verification equation into a linear one.

Module 7

Going Deeper

Where to go after you've built a foundation in PLONK.

PLONK Variants & Extensions

PLONK has spawned a family of improved proof systems:

TurboPLONK

Extends PLONK with custom gates (more than just add/multiply) for operations like hash functions and range checks. Reduces circuit sizes dramatically for common operations.

UltraPLONK

Adds lookup tables (PLOOKup) on top of TurboPLONK. Instead of building everything from arithmetic gates, you can "look up" precomputed values — massive efficiency gains for bitwise operations.

HyperPLONK

Replaces univariate polynomials with multilinear polynomials over the boolean hypercube. Avoids the need for FFTs and opens up new optimization possibilities.

PLONK + FRI

Replaces KZG commitments with FRI (Fast Reed-Solomon IOP), eliminating the trusted setup. Used in systems like Plonky2 and Plonky3 by Polygon.

The Bigger Ecosystem

Understanding PLONK is a gateway to the broader ZK ecosystem:

  • zkEVMs: Systems like zkSync, Polygon zkEVM, and Scroll use PLONK-based provers to verify Ethereum transactions
  • zkVMs: General-purpose virtual machines (like RISC Zero, SP1) that can prove arbitrary computation
  • Recursive proofs: Using PLONK to verify PLONK proofs, enabling proof aggregation and incrementally verifiable computation
  • Folding schemes: Nova, Sangria — alternative approaches to incrementally verifiable computation
From the learning path: "I did not finish the path fully, going through this made me appreciate proving abstractions even more like zkVM/zkEVMs :)" — Understanding what PLONK does under the hood makes you a better user and builder of ZK systems, even if you work at a higher abstraction level.
